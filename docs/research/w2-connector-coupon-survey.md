# W2 connector + coupon implementation survey — snap-fit numbers, detents, keyholes, warp

*Research date: 2026-07-28. Prepared as Appendix-A source material for the W2 design doc.*

Scope note: this survey deliberately does not revisit ground already covered by `docs/research/tile-craft-field-survey.md` (fit-clearance ladder, tile-leveling market landscape, garage-tile and PVC-panel ecosystems, anchor ratings, Dzus/quarter-turn fasteners as a hardware class) or `docs/research/tile-wall-grounding-audit.md`. ## 1. Cantilever snap-fit engineering numbers

**Primary source, fetched and read in full: the Bayer/Covestro "Snap-Fit Joints for Plastics — A Design Guide"** ([Covestro PDF](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)). Its cantilever formulas (Table 1, rectangular cross-section):

- Permissible deflection, constant cross-section: **y = 0.67 · ε_pm · l² / h** (ε_pm = permissible strain, l = arm length, h = root thickness) ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)).
- Thickness tapered to h/2 at the tip: **y = 1.09 · ε_pm · l² / h** — the guide notes the tapered arm permits "more than 60%" additional deflection at the same root strain ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)).
- Width tapered to b/4 at the tip: **y = 0.86 · ε_pm · l² / h** ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)).
- Deflection force: **P = (b·h²/6) · (E_s·ε / l)**, using the **secant modulus** E_s at the actual strain, not Young's modulus ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)).

**Permissible strain rules** (same guide): for one-time assembly, semi-crystalline polymers may be taken to about the yield strain; amorphous polymers to about **70% of yield strain**; glass-filled grades to about **half the elongation at break**; and for **frequently separated/rejoined** joints, use only about **60%** of those values ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)). Its Table 2 short-term permissible strains at 23 °C: Makrolon PC 4%, Bayblend PC/ABS 2.5%, Makroblend 3.5%, Apec 4%, glass-filled Makrolon 2.0–2.2% ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)). The guide also states that after joining, snap-fit features should return to an essentially **stress-free condition** — retention must not depend on sustained strain ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)). For torsion snap arms, permissible shear strain γ_pm ≈ **1.35 · ε_pm** ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)).

**Mating/separation force**: W = P · (μ + tan α) / (1 − μ · tan α), with α the entry lead angle for mating and the return angle α′ for separation; the guide's friction table gives plastic-on-steel values (PC 0.45–0.55) multiplied by ~1.2 for like-on-like plastic pairs ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)). The same W equation appears in the **BASF-lineage** treatment summarized by Machine Design: maximum dynamic strain **ε = 1.5·t·Y / (L²·Q)** and force **P = b·t²·E·ε₀ / (6·L·Q)**, where **Q is the deflection magnification factor** accounting for rotation of the compliant base wall — significant at low L/t aspect ratios (worked example: 30% glass-filled PET at 1.5% allowable strain) ([Machine Design](https://www.machinedesign.com/archive/article/21818595/new-equations-make-fastening-plastic-components-a-snap)). The BASF manual itself could not be fetched directly (see access notes); the Q-factor charts are in the manual and should be consulted via a library copy before relying on exact Q values.

**Source of the "root fillet ≥ 0.5×t" rule**: the Covestro guide's stress-concentration chart shows the concentration factor falling steeply with R/h and flattening around **R/h ≈ 0.6**, with a stated minimum root radius of **0.015 in (~0.4 mm)** and a caution that oversized fillets cause sink marks ([Covestro](https://solutions.covestro.com/-/media/covestro/solution-center/brands/downloads/imported/1556891135.pdf)). The FDM restatement is Hubs' snap-fit guide: use "a fillet radius of at least 0.5 times the thickness of the cantilever base" ([Hubs, vendor knowledge base](https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-snap-fit-joints-3d-printing/)). So the rule is Bayer/BASF injection-molding doctrine carried into FDM practice, not an FDM-specific derivation.

**FDM-specific adjustments** (vendor/community-grade): Hubs recommends minimum cantilever base ~1 mm, minimum width ~5 mm, ~0.5 mm assembly clearance, and — critically for W2 — **never orienting a snap arm so it flexes across layer lines**; arms must lie in the XY print plane, and brittle PLA is a poor snap material versus PETG ([Hubs snap-fit](https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-snap-fit-joints-3d-printing/), [Hubs FDM design](https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-parts-fdm-3d-printing/), [Formlabs, vendor](https://formlabs.com/blog/designing-3d-printed-snap-fit-enclosures/)). Unionfab (vendor) quantifies anisotropy: Z-oriented FDM parts lose roughly **50% of elongation** and 20–30% of tensile strength versus XY, and it suggests halving allowable strain for any Z-loaded feature; it lists elongation at break PLA ~8% and PETG ~24% (datasheet-class bulk values) while recommending printed-part **design strain limits of ~5–8% for PLA and ~7–10% for PETG** ([Unionfab, vendor](https://www.unionfab.com/blog/2025/06/3d-print-snap-fit)). Note the disagreement between datasheet elongation (PETG ~24%) and working design strain (7–10%): the lower figures are the ones consistent with the Covestro 60%-for-rejoining rule and should govern. PETG's superior layer adhesion for load-bearing hooks (interlayer strength ~30% above PLA) is a vendor claim from Qidi ([Qidi, vendor](https://qidi3d.com/blogs/print-lab/pla-petg-heavy-wall-hook-material-guide)).

## 2. Bayonet / twist-lock detent geometry

**Rotation angles.** Firgelli's bayonet-joint engineering guide (vendor-grade but the most quantitative accessible source) says **90° is the standard rotation** — "use 90° unless there is a reason not to" — with **60° used on small fittings** such as BNC connectors ([Firgelli](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/bayonet-joint)). Printed short-twist precedent exists well below that: the SnapLock parametric twist-lock enclosure uses a **20° twist** with **4 tabs at 90° spacing** ([SnapLock, GitHub, community](https://github.com/flight505/SnapLock)). Camera bayonet mounts use 3–4 tabs, keyed asymmetrically so the lens cannot be inserted mis-clocked, locked by a spring-loaded pin ([Wikipedia — Lens mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_mount), [Wikipedia — Bayonet mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayonet_mount)); specific per-brand rotation angles were not verifiable from the pages fetched and are left unstated (unverified).

**Pin/lug and clearance norms** (Firgelli, vendor): two opposed pins of **⌀1.5–3 mm** (up to 6 mm for heavy joints), **0.1–0.2 mm** radial clearance between pin and slot, and **0.3–0.5 mm corner radii** on slot corners to avoid stress concentration ([Firgelli](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/bayonet-joint)).

**Detent depth — the key number, converging from two independent sources**: Firgelli states that **"0.3–0.5 mm of axial relief gives a clear tactile click"** at the end of slot travel, held by spring preload of **5–20 N** (a BA15s lamp bayonet holds ~15 N) ([Firgelli](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/bayonet-joint)). SnapLock independently uses detent **columns protruding 0.3–0.5 mm** (⌀0.8 mm columns riding into ⌀1.2 mm notches, with a fillet on the column tip) to produce the click ([SnapLock, community](https://github.com/flight505/SnapLock)). What makes a printed detent click rather than shear: a small (≤0.5 mm) rounded, filleted bump; deliberate size mismatch between bump and notch so contact stays compliant; a **5° entry clearance** on the engaging faces; and printing so the flexing member lies in the XY plane with self-supporting 45° chamfers under overhung tabs ([SnapLock](https://github.com/flight505/SnapLock), [Hubs](https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-snap-fit-joints-3d-printing/)).

**Printed bayonet at high fit quality** (community, expert-grade): Nick Sherlock's reverse-engineered printed lens mounts use three lugs with a **1.2 mm axial lug drop**, assume **±0.05 mm** printer accuracy, add **0.3 mm chamfers** under lugs for printability and lead-in, put **1 mm fillets at lug roots** specifically to prevent lug shear, and tune fit by scaling for ±0.2% material shrinkage ([nicksherlock.com](https://www.nicksherlock.com/2024/01/reverse-engineering-lens-mounts-for-3d-printing/)).

**Anti-rattle**: in metal bayonets a spring provides continuous axial preload so lugs stay seated ([Wikipedia — Lens mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_mount), [Firgelli](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/bayonet-joint)); in an all-printed joint the elastic arm itself must supply this preload, which is exactly the past-center detent role in the W2 clip — the arm relaxes into the detent pocket (near-stress-free at rest, per the Covestro principle in §1) while retaining a small residual bias. Quarter-turn (Dzus-class) hardware background is already covered in the prior field survey and is not repeated here.

## 3. Keyhole slot dimensions and face-down printing

**Screw head dimensions (ASME B18.6.3 values via accessible mirrors).** 82° slotted flat (countersunk) heads: **#6 head ⌀ 0.262/0.238 in max/min (6.65/6.05 mm), height 0.083 in (2.11 mm); #8 ⌀ 0.312/0.285 in (7.92/7.24 mm), height 0.100 in (2.54 mm)** ([GlobalFastener B18.6.3 table](https://www.globalfastener.com/standards/detail_4972.html)). Slotted pan heads: **#6 ⌀ 0.270 in (6.86 mm), height 0.082 in (2.08 mm); #8 ⌀ 0.322 in (8.18 mm), height 0.096 in (2.44 mm)** ([Engineers Edge](https://www.engineersedge.com/pan_head1.htm)). 100°/82° flat head reference diameters: #6 0.279 in, #8 0.332 in ([Engineers Edge](https://www.engineersedge.com/phillips_flat_head.htm)); a vendor countersink chart lists #6 → 0.279 in, #8 → 0.322 in countersink diameter ([Rivcut, vendor](https://www.rivcut.com/resources/countersink-chart)). Nominal thread major diameters: **#6 = 0.138 in (3.51 mm), #8 = 0.164 in (4.17 mm)** ([AFT Fasteners, vendor chart](https://aftfasteners.com/machine-screws-dimensions-mechanical-specs/)).

**Keyhole hardware norms.** A representative steel keyhole mounting plate specifies a mounting opening of **23/64 in (9.13 mm)**, is driven by #6 flat-head screws, and is rated 30 lb ([Craftparts, vendor](https://www.craftparts.com/products/1-3-4-keyhole-mounting-plate)); the woodworking-standard keyhole router bit cuts a **3/8 in (9.5 mm)** entry ([Rockler, vendor](https://www.rockler.com/hanging-slot-router-bits-router-bits)). The Ideal Security published keyhole dimension sheet could not be fetched (Cloudflare block — access failure). A popular printable keyhole part uses **⌀8 mm entry, 4 × 8 mm slot, ~3 mm head cavity**, reporting #6 fits and #8 is tight, with advice to scale up for #8 ([Printables model 146312, community — page fetch blocked, details from search summaries and mirror](https://www.printables.com/model/146312-keyhole-slot-for-wall-hanging)). A parametric keyhole-slot negative generator exists on MakerWorld (listing only; page fetch blocked) ([MakerWorld 1624405](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1624405-keyhole-mounting-slot-negative-part-generator)).

**Cross-check against the W2 spec**: ⌀9.5 mm entry clears an #8 pan head (8.18 mm) by ~1.3 mm and matches the 3/8 in hardware norm ([Engineers Edge](https://www.engineersedge.com/pan_head1.htm), [Rockler](https://www.rockler.com/hanging-slot-router-bits-router-bits)); a 4.7–5.0 mm slot clears the #8 major diameter (4.17 mm) by 0.5–0.8 mm ([AFT Fasteners](https://aftfasteners.com/machine-screws-dimensions-mechanical-specs/)); head cavity depth of ~3 mm covers the #8 pan head height (2.44 mm) plus clearance, agreeing with the community part's ~3 mm ([Printables 146312, community](https://www.printables.com/model/146312-keyhole-slot-for-wall-hanging)).

**FDM bridging norms for the cavity roof (tile printed face-down, so the keyhole cavity ceiling is a bridge).** Sources disagree on maximum span: Hydra Research's design rules say keep bridges **≤10 mm** ([Hydra Research](https://www.hydraresearch3d.com/design-rules)); UltiMaker's design guide allows ~10 mm for standard materials and up to ~25 mm for tough PLA ([UltiMaker, vendor](https://ultimaker.com/learn/design-for-fff-3d-printing-maximize-your-success/)); community guidance reports clean bridges to ~10 mm and sagging but printable spans to ~30 mm ([Innostation, community](https://www.innostation3d.com/en/blogs/3d-college/bridging-and-overhangs-how-to-print-tricky-geometry-cleanly)). The conservative 10 mm figure comfortably covers a ⌀9.5 mm keyhole cavity. For horizontal cavities, established practice is a **teardrop (90° apex) or flat roof** instead of a circular ceiling, with the flat roof placed **~0.4 mm above the theoretical circle** to absorb first-bridge droop ([blog.rahix.de, community, detailed](https://blog.rahix.de/design-for-3d-printing/)). Two further techniques for clean stepped roofs: a **sacrificial bridge layer** spanning the whole cavity that is drilled/cut out after printing ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2017/10/17/sacrificial-bridge-avoids-3d-printed-supports/)), and **sequential bridging** — bridge a narrow band first, let 2–3 layers consolidate, then bridge perpendicular across the consolidated band to close a wider opening in stages ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2023/05/28/3d-printing-bores-without-support/), [blog.rahix.de](https://blog.rahix.de/design-for-3d-printing/)).

## 4. Warp and bow measurement methodology

**Methods, cheapest to most precise.** (a) Plate on a flat reference surface, steel straightedge across faces and diagonals, **feeler gauges** slipped under the gap — the standard shop method for bed and part flatness ([Sovol guide, vendor](https://sovol.eu/blogs/new/fix-warped-3d-printer-bed); feeler gauge background: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feeler_gauge)). (b) Dial-indicator or profile-scanning flatness/warpage measurement against a reference plane — the metrology-grade version ([Keyence application note, vendor](https://www.keyence.com/products/measure/applications/profile-measurement/flatness-and-warpage.jsp)). (c) The published FDM-specific protocol: Alsoufi & Elsayed measured **warp lift at each corner of a printed part with a digital height gauge**, reporting warp as a function of nozzle temperature and speed (optimum near 220 °C for PLA+ at low speed) — method verified from the paper's figure pages; the full text was not retrievable, so exact millimeter magnitudes from that study are **unverified** ([ResearchGate publication page](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318654219_Warping_Deformation_of_Desktop_3D_Printed_Parts_Manufactured_by_Open_Source_Fused_Deposition_Modeling_FDM_System), [method figure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/warping-deformation-and-its-method-of-measurement-at-each-corner_fig2_318654219)).

**Published magnitudes for flat plates are thin** (mostly community-grade): PETG is reported to contract ~0.5–0.7% on cooling versus ~0.3–0.5% for PLA, making PETG somewhat more warp-prone on large flat parts ([WhyItFailed, community](https://whyitfailed.fyi/blog/petg-warping-causes-and-how-to-fix-it)); Prusa forum users report large flat parts bowing **1–3 mm** even when they never released from the bed ([Prusa forum, community](https://forum.prusa3d.com/forum/original-prusa-i3-mk3s-mk3-how-do-i-print-this-printing-help/large-printed-parts-are-not-flat-even-though-they-dont-unstick-from-bed/)); Bambu-class PETG practice for flat parts is bed 80–85 °C, ~8 mm brim, auxiliary part fan off ([Bambu forum, community](https://forum.bambulab.com/t/petg-warping-on-a-new-p1s/85455/3)). The official Bambu wiki warping page could not be fetched (HTTP 402 — access failure). **No published measurement was found for the exact W2 case (100 × 100 × 4 mm PETG plate on textured PEI)**; the design doc's 0.2–0.5 mm working band is an inference consistent with the community reports above and must be replaced by measured values from the W2 warp coupon.

**Existing warp coupon designs** (all community; Printables pages partially blocked, details from search summaries): the Teaching Tech-style Warp Test ([Printables 1287384](https://www.printables.com/model/1287384-warp-test)), Bed Adhesion Warp Test ([Printables 4634](https://www.printables.com/model/4634-bed-adhesion-warp-test)), bed adhesion/warping strip test ([Printables 86721](https://www.printables.com/model/86721-bed-adhesion-warping-test)), customizable print-bed flatness tester ([Printables 281712](https://www.printables.com/model/281712-print-bed-flatness-tester-customizable)), heat-bed warping calibration print ([Printables 70284](https://www.printables.com/model/70284-heat-bed-warping-calibration-print)), and bed-warping temperature tests ([Printables 128948](https://www.printables.com/model/128948-bed-warping-temperature-tests-pla)). Common pattern: thin flat spans anchored by bulkier regions, printed without brim, with warp read as corner lift.

## 5. How printed wall systems join tiles (and the tile-leveling clip mechanism)

**Multiboard** joins adjacent tiles with printed snaps passing through shared edge holes: DS ("double-sided") snaps are two parts that snap in from opposite faces; quad snaps tie four tile corners at once; offset snaps add a 6.25 mm standoff and a screw hole so the same part both joins tiles and mounts them ([Multibuild docs — core parts](https://docs.multibuild.io/beginner-section/core-parts-documentation), [tile mounting guide](https://docs.multibuild.io/beginner-section/tile-mounting-guide)). The official parts are "designed with a tolerance of 0.25 mm" ([Thangs listing, vendor](https://thangs.com/designer/MultiBuild/3d-model/6.25%20mm%20-%20Quad%20Offset%20Snaps%20%28DS%20Part%20A%29-993369)), and the ecosystem distinguishes regular from weight-bearing snap classes ([Thangs listing](https://thangs.com/designer/Multiboard/3d-model/Honeycomb%20to%20Multiboard%20Weight%20Bearing%20Snap-1060980)); recommended practice is pre-assembling ~3×3 tile groups before wall mounting ([Multibuild docs](https://docs.multibuild.io/beginner-section/tile-mounting-guide)).

**openGrid** (28 mm grid; full boards 6.8 mm thick, lite 4 mm) joins tiles with **connector pegs dropped into edge holes**, and the official guide is explicit about their role: they are "primarily for alignment rather than structural support, though multiple connected boards do provide mutual reinforcement" — retention comes from screwing each board to the wall ([openGrid board guide](https://www.opengrid.world/guides/board/)). Fit is tolerance-sensitive (over-extrusion makes snaps refuse to engage), and the official advice for tight connectors is to reprint at **99.5% scale** ([openGrid board guide](https://www.opengrid.world/guides/board/), [openGrid snaps guide](https://www.opengrid.world/guides/snaps/)); a dedicated openConnect connector system exists (listing only; page fetch blocked) ([MakerWorld 2257440](https://makerworld.com/en/models/2257440-openconnect-opengrid-s-own-connector-system)).

**Honeycomb Storage Wall (HSW)** joins panel segments with printed 2-way and 4-way fasteners that double as the screw-mounting points; guidance is to mount through at least three corners with M3 screws and to "print one test insert first" before committing to a full set ([3DWithUs HSW guide, community](https://3dwithus.com/honeycomb-storage-wall-guide-3d-printed-tool-organization)).

**Gridfinity** is the counter-example: the spec attaches baseplates to the *surface* (6×2 mm magnets, M3 corner screws), not to each other ([gridfinity.xyz specification](https://gridfinity.xyz/specification/), [unofficial spec](https://github.com/gridfinity-unofficial/specification/blob/main/README.md)); plate-to-plate joining is a community retrofit via screw-together baseplates ([Printables 300603, community](https://www.printables.com/model/300603-gridfinity-screw-together-baseplate)) or clip-together baseplates whose clips add ~3.4 mm height ([MakerWorld 484160, community — page fetch blocked, details from search summaries](https://makerworld.com/en/models/484160-gridfinity-clip-together-baseplates)).

**Stiffness-vs-compliance lesson across all four**: every system separates *alignment* (pegs, lugs, keyed holes) from *retention* (snaps or screws), keeps the tile-to-tile joint compliant rather than rigid, sources panel stiffness from the wall behind the tiles, and ships a per-printer fit adjustment (0.25 mm design tolerance, 99.5% rescale, "print one test insert first") rather than pretending one tolerance fits all printers.

**Tile-leveling clip mechanism (geometry only — market covered in the prior survey).** The mechanism: a flat **base plate slides under both adjacent tiles**; a thin **breakaway strap rises through the joint** — the strap thickness doubles as the spacer, so a 1/16 in clip enforces a minimum 1/16 in joint ([PowerTec, vendor](https://powertecproducts.com/72107-tile-leveling-system-1-16-threaded-spacer-flat-base-strap-for-spacing-and-leveling-tiles-100-pk/), [Tools4Flooring Raimondi, vendor](https://www.tools4flooring.com/raimondi-1-16-clear-hd-tile-leveling-clips-250-bag.html)); a **wedge pushed through a slot in the strap, or a threaded cap screwed down the strap, bears on both tile top faces simultaneously**, clamping them coplanar against the shared base while the adhesive cures ([DIYTileGuy mechanism overview, community-expert](https://www.diytileguy.com/tile-leveling-systems/), [PowerTec](https://powertecproducts.com/72107-tile-leveling-system-1-16-threaded-spacer-flat-base-strap-for-spacing-and-leveling-tiles-100-pk/)). After cure the strap snaps off at a designed breakpoint by a kick or mallet blow **parallel to the joint**; wedges are reusable, clips consumable ([DIYTileGuy](https://www.diytileguy.com/tile-leveling-systems/)). Clips are sized to tile thickness ranges (e.g. XL clips for 12–20 mm tile ([Frosch, vendor](https://froschproducts.com/products/tile-leveling-clips-1-16-1-5mm-xl-clips-for-tile-thickness-12mm-1-2-to-20mm-3-4-2000pcs)); thick-tile clips for 1/8–1/2 in ([Contractors Direct Raimondi, vendor](https://www.contractorsdirect.com/products/raimondi-rls-clips-thick-tile))), and the recurring failure mode in user reviews is edge chipping during strap break-off ([Amazon listing reviews, community](https://www.amazon.com/Tile-2000pcs-1-5mm-Professional-Installation/dp/B0C9MRG59C)). The transferable principle: **coplanarity across a joint is achieved by clamping both front faces to one shared reference spanning the joint** — not by butting edges.

## 6. three.js InstancedMesh for the tile-wall preview

The official documentation describes InstancedMesh as "a special version of Mesh with instanced rendering support. Use InstancedMesh if you have to render a large number of objects with the same geometry and material(s) but with different world transformations," reducing the render to a single draw call ([three.js docs — InstancedMesh](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/InstancedMesh.html)). Key API facts from the same page: `count` sets how many instances render; per-instance transforms go through `setMatrixAt(index, matrix)` and require **`instanceMatrix.needsUpdate = true`** after changes (likewise `instanceColor` for `setColorAt`); `boundingSphere` defaults to `null`; and `computeBoundingSphere()` carries the warning that the engine computes the bound automatically "for ray casting or view frustum culling" and "you may need to recompute the bounding sphere if an instance is transformed via .setMatrixAt()"; `dispose()` must be called to free the instanced attributes ([three.js docs — InstancedMesh](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/InstancedMesh.html), [source: src/objects/InstancedMesh.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/src/objects/InstancedMesh.js)).

**The frustum-culling pitfall**: culling is evaluated for the whole InstancedMesh against one bounding sphere, never per instance ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/how-to-do-frustum-culling-with-instancedmesh/22633)); when `setMatrixAt` moves instances outside the original bound, the entire mesh visibly vanishes at certain camera angles — the fixes are calling `computeBoundingSphere()` after updating matrices, or setting `frustumCulled = false` ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/solved-instancedmesh-dissapeared-because-of-frustum/53651)). A related trap: computing bounds while `count` is 0 yields an empty bounding sphere (radius −1) that never renders ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/problems-with-instanced-mesh-not-displaying-anything/62416/2)).

**Raycasting**: intersections against an InstancedMesh carry `instanceId` — "the index number of the instance where the ray intersects the InstancedMesh" — so per-tile picking needs no extra bookkeeping ([three.js docs — Raycaster](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/Raycaster.html); raycast implementation in [src/objects/InstancedMesh.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/src/objects/InstancedMesh.js)).

**When it is worth it at N ≤ ~50**: the case for instancing is draw-call overhead, which dominates only at large object counts ([threejsroadmap.com, community](https://threejsroadmap.com/blog/draw-calls-the-silent-killer)); conversely, a tracked three.js issue documents scenarios where InstancedMesh renders *slower* than plain Meshes — few instances with heavyweight geometry — because instancing forfeits per-object culling and adds per-instance attribute overhead ([three.js GitHub issue #30352](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/30352)). For a 2×2-to-modest wall of identical tiles (N = 4–50) either approach performs fine; instancing's practical advantage is structural — one shared tile BufferGeometry, one material, per-tile placement as matrices — while N plain meshes win if tiles ever need distinct materials or per-tile culling.

## Implications for the W2 design

1. **Clip arm sizing (§1)**: taper the bayonet arm thickness toward h/2 at the tip — Covestro's y = 1.09·ε·l²/h buys >60% more deflection at the same root strain versus a constant arm. Budget design strain for printed PETG at **≤ ~2%** effective: start from the 7–10% vendor design band, apply Covestro's 0.6 factor for a joint that is repeatedly separated, then halve again if any flexure crosses layer planes — and avoid that entirely by keeping both clip arms and clipseat lips in the XY print plane. Keep the **root fillet ≥ 0.5× arm thickness** (Hubs rule, grounded in Covestro's R/h ≈ 0.6 knee, absolute minimum 0.4 mm). Use W = P(μ+tanα)/(1−μtanα) with a ~30° entry lead and a steeper return angle to make insertion light and retention firm, and shape the past-center detent so the arm is **near stress-free when seated** (Covestro's explicit requirement) with only a small residual bias for anti-rattle.
2. **Twist and detent (§2)**: the W2 ~30° twist sits below the 90° hardware norm but squarely in printed practice (SnapLock locks in 20° with 4 tabs). Adopt the twice-independently-confirmed detent number: bump **0.3–0.5 mm proud, rounded and filleted**, mating into a slightly larger pocket; add ~5° entry clearance on the X-bridge blade faces, 0.1–0.2 mm radial clearance in the clipseat, ~1 mm fillets at blade roots (Sherlock's anti-shear rule), and 0.3 mm chamfers under any overhung lug edges.
3. **Keyhole (§3)**: the spec'd ⌀9.5 mm entry / 4.7–5.0 × 8 mm slot / ≥4 mm collar is confirmed against primary dimensions — #8 pan head ⌀8.18 mm and major ⌀4.17 mm clear with sensible margins, and 9.5 mm matches the 3/8 in hardware convention. Make the head cavity ~3 mm deep (2.44 mm head + clearance). For the face-down print, the cavity ceiling spans ≤ ~9.5 mm — inside the conservative 10 mm bridge rule — but design it as a **flat or stepped roof with ~0.4 mm droop allowance**, closing the 9.5 → 5 mm transition in two sequential-bridging stages; include the keyhole in the coupon so both roof variants get printed and inspected.
4. **Warp coupon (§4)**: no published number exists for a 100×100×4 mm PETG plate on textured PEI, so W2 must generate its own: print the plate (both rebate variants), set it on a flat reference, and record straightedge + feeler-gauge gaps at four corners and center plus diagonals — the Alsoufi-style per-corner protocol at shop-tool cost. Treat the current 0.2–0.5 mm band as a placeholder consistent with community reports (large flat parts up to 1–3 mm when printed badly), and keep the rule that clip capture depth ≥ 2× *measured* warp. Print PETG plates at bed 80–85 °C with a ~8 mm brim and aux fan off.
5. **Joint philosophy (§5)**: the ecosystem consensus validates the W2 architecture — registration features align, the clip retains, the screw carries load into the wall (openGrid states this division outright). Copy two operational habits: publish a per-printer fit adjustment path (Multiboard designs to 0.25 mm; openGrid tells users to rescale connectors 99.5%; HSW says print one test insert first) — the clip coupon is exactly this artifact — and take the tile-leveling clip's core lesson: the clip jaws bearing on both tiles' **front border bands** clamp both faces to one shared reference across the joint, which is the proven way to get coplanarity; keep all compliance in the replaceable clip, none in the tile.
6. **Preview rendering (§6)**: for the 4-tile W2 preview (and up to ~50 tiles), use one InstancedMesh over the shared tile BufferGeometry: `setMatrixAt` per tile, then `instanceMatrix.needsUpdate = true` **and `computeBoundingSphere()`** (or `frustumCulled = false`) to dodge the disappearing-mesh pitfall; use the intersection's `instanceId` for tile picking. Fall back to N plain meshes only if tiles diverge in material or geometry — at this count the performance difference is negligible either way.
